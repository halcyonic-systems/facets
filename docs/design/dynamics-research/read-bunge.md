## 1. State, state space, lawful state space — the formal definitions

**VERIFIED**, Ch.1 §2.2 "The State Space Representation" (lines 627–664).

Bunge builds the state-space apparatus in stages:

1. **State function.** For a kind of system `K` with `n` general properties, each property is a function `F_i: A → V_i`. Collected into an ordered tuple:
   `𝔽 = ⟨F_1, F_2, …, F_n⟩ : A → V_1 × V_2 × … × V_n` (line 629–631)
   `A` (the domain) is a cartesian product of the system kind `K`, the power set of environmental items `2^E`, the set of reference frames `F`, the set of time instants `T`, etc. (line 635). So state is *always* indexed by an environment-coupling and a reference frame, not just by the system in isolation — VERIFIED, this is explicit in the domain construction, not incidental.

2. **State.** `𝔽(k, e, f, t, …) = ⟨a, b, …, n⟩ ∈ ℝⁿ` is *the state of `k` at `t`* (line 640).

3. **Conceivable vs. lawful state space.** The set of all possible tuples is the *conceivable* state space `S(K)`. Because the components of `𝔽` are "lawfully interrelated, and thus mutually restricted," not every n-tuple is *really* (nomologically) possible — the **lawful state space** `S_L(K)` is a proper subset of `S(K)` (line 640). This distinction is load-bearing for the whole account: laws don't just *evolve* points in state space, they first *carve out* which points are even admissible.

4. **System vs. aggregate test via state space.** "A thing is an aggregate if and only if its state space equals the union of the state spaces of its components — otherwise it is a (concrete) system" (line 650). This is the formal payoff of the whole apparatus (Cf. Bunge 1977a, 1977b) — and it is a *structural/compositional* criterion, not a dynamical one per se, though it's built out of the same state-space machinery dynamics uses.

5. **Compressed semantic assumption**, items (i)–(x), lines 660–680 — this is the single passage that answers essentially your whole brief in one block. Verbatim structure (I'm compressing his prose, not paraphrasing the math):
   - (iii) state of a `K` at `a`: `s = 𝔽(a) = ⟨F_1(a), …, F_n(a)⟩`
   - (iv) **lawful state space** `S_L(K)` = the *range* of `𝔽` (not the codomain — the actually-achieved image). Worth flagging: this differs subtly from the earlier "proper subset of conceivable space carved out by laws" framing — here it's defined as the range of the state function itself, which folds the lawfulness into the definition of `𝔽` rather than treating it as a subsequent restriction. Bunge doesn't reconcile the two phrasings explicitly (UNVERIFIED whether he sees them as equivalent by construction or whether this is looseness in the text).
   - (v)/(vi) **event**: an ordered triple `⟨s, s′, g⟩` where `s, s′ ∈ S_L(K)` and `g: S_L(K) → S_L(K)` is a lawful map. **Event space** `E_L(K)` = the collection of all such lawful events.
   - (viii) **process**: for time-indexed `𝔽: T → ℝⁿ`, the totality of processes in `x` during interval `τ ⊆ T` is `π(x, τ) = {𝔽(t) | t ∈ τ}` — literally the *set of states visited*, not an ordered sequence.
   - (ix) **history**: `h(x) = {⟨t, 𝔽(t)⟩ | t ∈ τ}` — the *graph* of the state function, i.e., time-tagged trajectory. This is the formal object: history = trajectory (a set of `(time, state)` pairs), not a narrative or a hierarchy.
   - (x) **total action/effect** of `x` on `y`: `A(x,y) = h(y|x) ∩ h̄(y)` — the difference between `y`'s forced trajectory (with `x` acting) and its free trajectory (counterfactual without `x`). This is Bunge's formal handle on causal influence, defined entirely in terms of trajectory-difference in state space.

## 2. Event as ordered-pair/triple transition; process as chain of events

**VERIFIED**, lines 652–658 (Ch.1 §2.2, prose leading into the formal block above).

> "Every event occurs in or to some concrete thing and it consists in a change of state of the thing... A light flash, the dissociation of a molecule, a storm, the growth of a bud, the learning of a trick, and the fall of a government are events — **or rather processes, for they are complex and therefore analyzable into further events.** Being changes in the states of things, events and processes are representable as **trajectories** in the state spaces of changing things." (line 652)

Key formal moves (line 654, Fig. 1.6):
- A **lawful event or process** with endpoints `s, s′` is representable by the triple `⟨s, s′, g⟩` where `g: S_L(K) → S_L(K)` must be *compatible with the laws of the K's* — lawfulness is a first-class constraint on the transition function itself, not an afterthought.
- If you **disregard intermediate states**, you're left with the **ordered pair** `⟨s, s′⟩ ∈ S_L(K) × S_L(K)` — the "net event." The set of all such pairs (for a given `g`) is the **event space**.
- `E_L(K) ⊆ S_L(K) × S_L(K)`, and in general the inclusion is *proper*: **not all state transitions are lawful** (line 658). This is exactly the "which transitions are admissible" question — Bunge gives it a name and a home (the event space) but the *content* of which transitions are lawful is external, supplied by the laws of the specific kind `K`.
- **Different trajectories can share endpoints** (Fig. 1.6: `g ≠ g′` both go `s → s′`) — so the *net* event (the ordered pair) under-determines the *process* (the full triple with `g`). This distinction — net change vs. full process — is directly relevant to your DLG/FSA case: a state-machine transition diagram typically records only the net-event level (which state to which state, labeled by input), collapsing the "how" (`g`) into a symbol, whereas a conservation-flow simulation is explicitly tracking `g` (the actual curve/rate).

Process as "chain of events": the text says events/processes are "analyzable into further events" (line 652) and the automaton definitions in the Appendix formalize this as *sequences*: "the internal states form sequences, i.e. the effect of a compound input `x∘y`... equals the effect of the second input acting on the automaton in the state to which the first input carried it: `M(s, x∘y) = M(M(s,x), y)`" (Definition A2(x), lines 4279–4282). This is literally chain-composability of transitions, formalized for the discrete/automaton case specifically — it isn't stated as a general definition of "process" back in Ch.1, but it *instantiates* the Ch.1 process concept in the automaton formalism.

## 3. History — the formal object

**VERIFIED.** Answered above (line 673–676): `h(x) = {⟨t, 𝔽(t)⟩ | t ∈ τ}` — a **set of time-state pairs**, i.e., the graph of the state trajectory. Confirmed generically at line 1839: "The history of any system can be represented as the trajectory of its representative point in a suitable state space for the system" — and reused verbatim across domains:
- Chemistry (line 1228): "the full trajectory of the state point `𝔽(t)` in `S_L(K)`... is called the **total history** of `σ`."
- Biology/development (line 1839): life history = a line in state space; "development" = the birth-to-adult segment of that line.
- Evolution (line 1962, Definition 3.13): "The evolution of a biopopulation **is** the history of the latter" — evolution is not a *separate* concept, it's history specialized to biopopulations. This is a strong instance of Bunge's method: don't invent new primitives per domain, *instantiate* the state-space/history primitive.
- Social systems (lines 3929–3938): social history is defined as a *matrix* `H = ‖H_ik‖` of per-cell histories — still trajectories, just indexed by a spatial/social partition in addition to time. Notably here Bunge explicitly flags the computational cost of this approach for realistic social systems ("a staggering amount of information... a staggering number of equations of evolution") and falls back to a coarser demographic method — an honest admission that the general state-space account, while formally uniform, is not always *tractable* (line 3803).

So: **history is univocally the trajectory (graph of the state function over an interval)**, reused identically from chemistry to biology to society to (implicitly) cognition. This is the single most load-bearing piece of evidence that Bunge intends this as a *generic* account, not a physics-specific one dressed up in general language.

## 4. How laws constrain trajectories

**VERIFIED**, synthesizing lines 640, 654, 658, 1221.

Laws operate at two distinct levels in Bunge's account, which is important for your instrument-design question (what does an "admissible transition" mean):

1. **Which points count as states at all** — the lawful state space `S_L(K)` is a proper subset of the conceivable/coordinate space `S(K)` (line 640). A point can be geometrically expressible (an n-tuple of reals) and still be *nomologically impossible* — never occupied by any real system of that kind.
2. **Which transitions between admissible states count as events** — the event space `E_L(K)` is a proper subset of `S_L(K) × S_L(K)` (line 658): even between two lawful states, not every pair is a lawful transition. The transition-function `g` itself must be "compatible with the laws of the system(s) concerned" (line 654).

He makes the double-restriction explicit with a memorable line: laws let us "allow only lawful events and discard lawless ones, i.e. **miracles**" (line 654).

Chemistry gives a worked instance (line 1221): the *bundle* of all lawful trajectories, across all initial conditions and all systems of a kind, "represents the totality `𝕃` of laws 'at work' in chemical systems" — i.e., the extension of the lawful-trajectory-bundle just *is* (extensionally) the content of the laws. This is a clean statement that Bunge's laws are behaviorally cashed out as constraints on trajectory sets — not separately-stated axioms that happen to correlate with trajectories, but definitionally identified with them at the extensional level.

## 5. Substrate-neutrality — discrete state machines, continuous flows, stochastic change

This is the crux of your brief, so I'll go point by point with direct textual support.

### (a) Continuous flows — VERIFIED, well-covered.
The Ch.1 §2.2 apparatus is built with real-valued codomains (`𝔽: A → ℝⁿ`, line 631) and continuous-time examples throughout: kinetic theory of gases (pressure/volume/temperature cube), Hamiltonian dynamics (phase space `⟨q,p⟩`), chemical kinetics (concentration hypercube), population genetics (gene-frequency space) — all line 644. Appendix A (line 4117) explicitly covers continuous transfer-function systems ("differentiator," "delay line") with `S = U × V`. This is squarely bert-compose's conservation-flow world: `𝔽` = the conserved quantities, `g` = the flow law, trajectory = the simulated run.

### (b) Discrete state machines (FSA/DLG) — VERIFIED, but in a *separate, only loosely-integrated* formalism.
Appendix A §2.2–2.3 (lines 4269–4417) gives a full axiomatic treatment of the **finite deterministic automaton**:
`𝒜 = ⟨Σ, Ω, σ₀, ∘, S, M, N, s₀, F⟩` (Definition A2, line 4269), with `S` "called the state space of `A`" (line 4273, clause iv) — Bunge *reuses the term* "state space" for the automaton's finite state set. The transition function is `M: S × Σ* → S` (clause v), and sequential composition is exactly the chain/process idea from Ch.1: `M(s, x∘y) = M(M(s,x), y)` (clause x, line 4279–4282).

**Important honest gap I need to flag**: Bunge does *not*, anywhere in the text I found, explicitly identify the automaton's `S` with the Ch.1 `S_L(K)` construction, or show `M` is an instance of the general lawful-map `g: S_L(K) → S_L(K)` from line 654. The two formalisms — the Ch.1 body-text "semantic assumption" (state function, event, process, history) and the Appendix A automaton theory — are presented as parallel mathematical models ("particular, yet also cross-disciplinary, mathematical models of systems," line 625) rather than as one being derived from or unified with the other. The Appendix's own framing statement: "Nothing is said about the precise structure of the system, let alone its physics and chemistry: this is why automata theory has such a wide range of applications" (line 4284) — this is Bunge advertising automata theory's generality *on its own terms*, not cashing it out as a special case of the Ch.1 state-space ontology.

So: **the automaton IS covered, but as a sibling formalism under the same "state space" umbrella-term, not as a formally-derived specialization of the Ch.1 event/process/history definitions.** For your instrument this matters: if you want "dynamics = trajectory through a lawful state space, transitions constrained by laws" as your unifying kernel-level answer, you get to *assert* that FSA/DLG transitions are a discretized instance of `g: S_L → S_L` — Bunge gives you the conceptual room to do so (both use "state," "state space," "transition," lawfulness-as-admissibility) but he does not do the unification work for you. That's a genuine synthesis step you'd be adding, not something you can cite him for verbatim.

Probabilistic automata (Definition A6, line 4346) extend `M` to a stochastic matrix `M: S × Σ → [0,1]^(n+1)` — deterministic automata are recovered as the `r=0` cutpoint limit of probabilistic ones (Theorem A6, line 4413), and Bunge states the general metatheorem "every stochastic theory includes a deterministic subtheory" (line 4416, flagged by him as *unproven* — "has no known proof"). Relevant to your Boolean-network deterministic/out-degree-1 case: it sits as the zero-noise limit of the probabilistic-automaton formalism, which Bunge does explicitly relate back to the deterministic automaton (Theorem A6) — this piece of the unification *is* done by him, unlike the Ch.1↔automaton link above.

### (c) Stochastic change — VERIFIED, present in two places, not unified into one probability treatment.
1. Appendix A probabilistic automata (above) — discrete, transition-probability matrices.
2. In the continuous/general-ontology text: "irregular" departures from equilibrium are called "**fluctuations or stochastic oscillations**" (line 4222) — but this is descriptive vocabulary in the equilibrium-and-stability discussion (Appendix, control-systems section), not a formal probabilistic extension of the `𝔽/S_L/g` apparatus from Ch.1. I found **no passage** generalizing the Ch.1 lawful-map `g` to a stochastic kernel the way Definition A6 does for automata specifically. So Bunge's stochastic treatment is real but **local to the automaton formalism**, not lifted to the general continuous state-space ontology. If your instrument needs "stochastic conservation flow" as a mode, Bunge doesn't hand you that generalization — you'd be extending him, again with room to do so (nothing in his definitions forbids `g` being a distribution rather than a function) but not textual support you can cite directly.

### Overall verdict on substrate-neutrality
**Honest assessment**: Bunge's state-space/event/process/history apparatus (Ch.1 §2.2) is genuinely substrate-neutral *in its stated form* — nothing in `𝔽: A → ℝⁿ`, `⟨s,s′,g⟩`, `π(x,τ)`, `h(x)` presumes continuity, determinism, or any particular domain (he instantiates it across physics, chemistry, biology, cognition, and society, each time by re-specifying `𝔽`'s components — this repetition across five+ domains is itself the strongest evidence for genericity). BUT: it is stated over `ℝⁿ`-valued state functions, and the discrete/automaton and stochastic/probabilistic material live in a **separate appendix formalism** that uses matching vocabulary ("state," "state space," "transition") without an explicit bridging theorem connecting the two. **The unification you need for bert-lenses — one kernel notion of "dynamics" covering conservation flow, FSA/DLG, Boolean-network trajectories, and stochastic change — is *suggested* by Bunge's parallel construction and shared vocabulary, but not *proven* or *executed* by him.** That's a place where your own theory work would need to do original synthesis, citable as "in Bunge's spirit / extending Bunge's parallel formalisms" rather than "per Bunge, definition X."

## 6. Levels and emergence — constraints on cross-level dynamics

**VERIFIED**, several distinct threads:

- **Levels have their own laws.** "Every level in the biosphere has its own laws: laws of cells, of organisms, of populations, and of ecosystems" (line 2300, stated as postulate S2). This directly implies that a lawful state space `S_L(K)` is *level-relative* — a component's `g`-map at the physical level need not determine (by itself) the `g`-map at the level above.
- **Reductionism is rejected as a general thesis, but not entirely**: Bunge holds "ontological pluralism (or emergentism)" *combined with* "moderate epistemological reductionism" (line 1580) — levels are ontologically real (new laws emerge) but higher-level phenomena are still explicable via lower-level mechanisms plus additional (not purely microphysical) hypotheses about component interaction (line 984: no macro-theory of water follows from micro-theory of H₂O molecules alone, even with bridge laws — you need hypotheses about the *interactions among components*).
- **Emergent vs. resultant property**, formalized concretely for societies (Definition, lines 3185–3186): a societal property `P` is *resultant* iff it is also a property of components (e.g., total food consumption — pure aggregation); it is *emergent* iff it is a societal property that is **not** resultant (e.g., family structure, political organization). This gives a crisp test, though it's stated for the social case specifically, not lifted to a fully general definition in the passages I found (UNVERIFIED whether an equally crisp general-case definition exists elsewhere in the Treatise outside what I read).
- **Emergence is temporal/novelty-based, not irreducibility-based** — per the vault's own concept distillation (`concepts/emergence.md`, citing "Bunge 1979 §1, p. 29", which I have not independently verified against the primary-source line numbers above — flagging as UNVERIFIED pending a direct page-29 check): emergent = new to the whole *at a time step* in the system's development. This framing is dynamical by construction — emergence is a change-of-state-space event (the system's admissible state space itself gaining a dimension/axis), which connects directly to the "qualitative vs quantitative change" passage at line 4026: "quantitative change... all of the axes of the state space... are used; in qualitative [change] some new axes pop up and others drop out." **This is Bunge's answer to state-space *reconfiguration* as opposed to mere movement within a fixed space** — directly relevant if your multi-timescale/hierarchy work needs a notion of a run that changes its own state-space dimensionality (e.g., mode transitions, structural learning in a Boolean network, or Facets' own "grounding" mode-shift work).
- **Cross-level dynamical constraint, not fully spelled out formally**: I did not find an explicit formal postulate of the form "the lawful state space of a higher-level system is *constructed from* (not merely correlated with) the lawful state spaces / histories of its component levels." The closest is the general system/aggregate criterion (line 650: total state space ≠ union of partial state spaces when components interact) plus the levels-have-own-laws postulate (line 2300) — together these imply cross-level dynamics can't be a naive product/composition of lower-level trajectories, but Bunge doesn't give you the composition rule itself. This is consistent with his own admission (line 3803, social case) that a fully worked-out multi-level state-space account becomes computationally intractable in practice and he falls back to coarser methods.

## 7. Assessment: is Bunge the strongest generic candidate, honestly?

**Strengths (for your convergence-thesis / kernel-design purposes):**
- The state-space/event/process/history stack is stated once, abstractly, and then genuinely *reused* verbatim across five+ domains (gas kinetics, Hamiltonian mechanics, chemistry, developmental biology, evolutionary biology, cognition, sociology) rather than restated per-domain — this repetition-without-modification is the best evidence of real generality, not just rhetorical claims to generality.
- The event/net-event distinction (`⟨s,s′,g⟩` vs. `⟨s,s′⟩`) gives you a principled place to hang "a DLG/FSA transition diagram only records net events; a conservation-flow run tracks the full `g`" — this is a real conceptual resource, not just terminology.
- History-as-trajectory-graph `h(x) = {⟨t,𝔽(t)⟩}` is a clean, substrate-neutral formal object that matches your kernel's `H` (History, not hierarchy) almost exactly in spirit — worth explicitly cross-checking against `Tuple.lean`'s `H` definition (not done in this pass; flagged as a follow-up, UNVERIFIED whether they align formally).
- Laws-as-trajectory-bundle-restriction (line 1221, `𝕃` = the bundle of lawful trajectories) gives a clean answer to "what does it mean for a transition to be admissible" that generalizes across your four target dynamics (conservation laws restrict flow trajectories; a DLG's transition table restricts which state-pairs are lawful; a Boolean network's update rule restricts trajectories to out-degree-1; an ABM's behavior rules restrict agent-trajectory bundles).

**Genuine limits (don't paper over these):**
- The discrete-automaton and probabilistic-automaton formalisms live in an **appendix**, presented as separate "mathematical models" sharing vocabulary with, but not formally derived from, the Ch.1 ontological apparatus. If you cite Bunge for "FSA transitions are dynamics in exactly my kernel's sense," you are making a synthesis move he does not make explicit — say so plainly in any downstream doc rather than smoothing it into "per Bunge."
- No general stochastic extension of the continuous state-space apparatus — stochasticity is handled only within the automaton formalism (Definition A6) and informally ("fluctuations," line 4222) elsewhere. If your instrument needs continuous stochastic dynamics (e.g., a Gillespie-style stochastic conservation process) as a first-class citizen, Bunge doesn't hand you the definitions; you'd be extending, not applying, his framework.
- Cross-level composition of state spaces/trajectories is asserted to be non-trivial (levels have own laws; total ≠ union of parts) but the actual *composition rule* connecting a lower-level history to an upper-level history is not given as a formal construction in the material I read — and Bunge himself flags the practical intractability of doing this rigorously even in his own worked social-systems example (line 3803).
- The `S_L(K)` = "proper subset of conceivable space, carved by laws" (line 640) vs. `S_L(K)` = "range of `𝔽`" (line 664, clause iv) phrasing tension noted in §1 above is a minor internal looseness worth being aware of if you formalize this in Lean — you'll need to pick one and state it precisely, since Bunge's own text doesn't force a single reading.

**Bottom line**: Bunge is the strongest textual anchor you have for a substrate-neutral dynamics vocabulary (state, state space, lawful subset, event, process, history, trajectory) and it is explicitly reused, not merely claimed general, across many domains including a real (if separately-housed) treatment of discrete automata and probabilistic transitions. It is *not*, as written, a single unified proof that flow-dynamics, FSA-dynamics, and stochastic dynamics are all instances of one formal object — that unification is compatible with what he wrote and strongly invited by his shared vocabulary and parallel constructions, but doing it rigorously (e.g., in Lean, or in your kernel's `T`) would be your contribution, not a restatement of his.
